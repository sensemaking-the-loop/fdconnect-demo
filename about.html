About this demonstration

A working copy of the system the Duke Center for Teaching & Learning uses to track faculty development work — running on invented people.

What you are looking at

Faculty Development Connect is an internal system built and maintained by the CTL faculty development team. It records the consultations, programs, and events the team runs with faculty, and it turns that record into something the team can actually use: a dashboard of activity, a searchable directory of everyone they work with, and a per-person dossier that reads like a document rather than a database row.

This site is that system, with the real people removed. Every name, title, department, engagement, date, and number was generated for this demonstration. You can click anything, filter anything, and submit the intake form. Nothing is saved and no real record exists behind any of it.

The distinction matters if you are evaluating whether to build something similar. The data here is fictional, but the categories, the intake flow, and the reporting are the ones a real team settled on and revised in use.

The four surfaces

  • Quick Log — the intake form. A consultant records an engagement in under a minute, on a phone if need be, without opening a spreadsheet.
  • Dashboards — activity by month, type, topic, school, and fiscal year, plus year-over-year comparison.
  • Directory — a searchable index of every contact, filterable by school, rank, program, staff member, fiscal year, recency, and how many times the team has worked with them.
  • Dossier — one page per person: their engagement history on a timeline, and a companion document where consultants write notes in their own words.

How it is put together

There is one system of record — a single spreadsheet — and everything else reads from it. Nothing keeps its own copy of the data, which is what stops two screens from disagreeing about the same person.

Intake is deliberately two-stage. The form collects what a consultant can recall quickly; a data manager then reviews each entry into the record, normalising the taxonomy as it lands. That human step is the reason the data is clean enough to chart, and it is the part most easily mistaken for inefficiency.

Notes live in documents rather than in cells, because a consultation is not well described by a field. The system creates and maintains the structure of each document; what goes inside it belongs to the person who did the work.

If you are considering building one

The honest version: this runs on Google Workspace and Apps Script, with no budget line and no engineering team. That is a real constraint and also the reason it exists — it was buildable by the people who needed it.

What it actually took was less about tooling than about two other things. A settled vocabulary, written down and owned by someone with the authority to decide it, so that "consultation" means one thing across every screen. And a named person responsible for the record, because a system of record with no custodian degrades quietly until nobody trusts what it reports.

What this demonstration does not show

  • The administrative surfaces — access control, the sync that maintains the dossier documents, and the data manager's own working views.
  • Access itself. The real system is limited to the team; here every door is open, which is why the intake form saves nothing.
  • Anything at the volume the real record carries. The demonstration population is smaller and tidier than a live year.
